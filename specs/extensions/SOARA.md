# The SOARA method
Situation, Objective, Action, Results, Aftermath

SOARA is a close cousin of the STAR method, used to structure behavioral interview answers (and by extension, resume bullet points) around a past challenge. It expands STAR's four steps into five: Situation (the challenge or context you faced), Objective (what you specifically set out to achieve, and why that was the goal), Action (what you personally did, including why you chose that approach over alternatives), Results (the outcome, and whether you met your stated objective), and Aftermath (what you learned from the experience and how you applied that learning afterward).

The two components that distinguish SOARA from STAR are Objective and Aftermath. Where STAR's "Task" briefly names the responsibility you were handed, SOARA's "Objective" asks you to be explicit about the specific goal you defined for yourself and the reasoning behind it, giving the interviewer a clearer target to measure your "Results" against. Aftermath has no STAR equivalent at all: it pushes the answer beyond the immediate result into longer-term impact, lessons learned, and whether you carried that learning into later situations. This makes SOARA less a rewording of STAR and more an extended version of it, asking for both sharper framing up front and reflection at the end.

SOARA is most associated with competency-based and graduate/campus recruiting, particularly in Australian and UK hiring practices, where interviewers use it to progressively draw out a candidate's problem-solving process and capacity for growth. It's a good fit when a role or interviewer explicitly values self-awareness and continuous improvement, since the Aftermath step rewards candidates who can show they reflected on and grew from an experience rather than just describing what happened. Unlike STAR, there is no widely-cited proportional breakdown (e.g., STAR's rough 20/10/60/10 split) for SOARA's five parts, but the same underlying principle applies: Action and Results should carry most of the narrative weight, with Situation and Objective kept brief enough to set up the story, and Aftermath kept to a short, concrete reflection rather than a generic lesson.
